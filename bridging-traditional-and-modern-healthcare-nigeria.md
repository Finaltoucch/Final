Bridging Traditional and Modern Healthcare: Reducing Delayed Hospital Presentation Linked to Herbal Medicine Use in Nigeria

Herbal medicine has been part of how Nigerians care for themselves for as long as anyone can remember. It is affordable. It is nearby. The person offering it usually speaks your language, knows your family, and does not ask for a deposit before treatment begins. That combination explains a great deal about why it has lasted.

So when illness starts, many people do not head first to a hospital. They go to a healer, or they boil something a neighbour recommended, or they buy a bottle at the market and give it a week or two. Often that is the end of the story and the person gets better. The trouble comes with the illnesses that do not wait. A breast lump that is still small in March can be a fungating mass by September. A diabetic foot ulcer that could have been dressed and saved ends as an amputation. A woman in obstructed labour loses hours she will never get back.

Research from Nigeria and from across the continent keeps landing on the same set of reasons for late presentation, and herbal treatment is only one of them. Money is a reason. Distance and bad roads are a reason. Fear of surgery, fear of hearing the word cancer, fear of what the hospital bill will do to the rest of the family, all of these are reasons. So is simply not knowing that a particular symptom is serious. Patients who arrive late tend to arrive with advanced disease, and what follows is a longer admission, harder treatment, a bigger bill, and a worse outcome than anyone wanted. We see it in breast cancer, in diabetic foot disease, in surgical emergencies, in severe infection, in obstetric complications.

It is tempting to respond to all this by campaigning against traditional medicine. I think that is the wrong instinct, and I think it fails in practice anyway. Telling a community that what their grandmothers used is worthless does not stop anybody using it. It only stops them telling the doctor about it. What we actually need is faster recognition of the small number of conditions that cannot afford to wait, and a clear, respected path from the healer's compound to the hospital gate.

A few things that would help.

Teach danger signs, not doctrine. Community education works far better when it is specific. A breast lump that does not go away. A wound on the foot of someone with diabetes. Bleeding in pregnancy. A swollen abdomen with vomiting and nothing passing. A child convulsing or too weak to feed. People remember concrete pictures. They forget general advice about the value of orthodox care.

Use the media people actually use. Radio in local languages, announcements in church and mosque, market days, WhatsApp voice notes, Nollywood storylines. Health messages travel through the channels people already trust, not the ones we wish they trusted.

Make the hospital worth reaching. Referral advice is hollow if the nearest primary health centre has no staff at night, no drugs on the shelf, and no way of moving a patient onward. Strengthening primary care and widening health insurance, so that a family is not choosing between treatment and rent, does more for early presentation than any amount of preaching.

Bring healers into the referral chain instead of routing around them. Train practitioners who are willing to be trained, so they can spot the conditions that need a hospital and hand those patients on quickly. Give them a named contact at the nearest facility rather than a leaflet. Recognise the referral publicly instead of treating it as a defeat. A healer who refers early keeps their standing in the community, and the patient keeps their life.

None of this asks traditional medicine to disappear. It asks both systems to stop pretending the other one is not there. Nigeria is not going to choose between them, so the useful question is how they talk to each other, and how quickly a patient can move from one to the other when the situation changes.

Get that right and a lot of avoidable deaths simply stop happening. Patients arrive earlier. Treatment is simpler and cheaper. Hospitals see better results. And people start to trust a system that has treated their culture with respect rather than contempt. For a country working with limited resources, that is about as practical as health reform gets.

The goal was never to replace traditional medicine with modern medicine. It is to make sure every patient receives the right care at the right time, through collaboration, timely referral, and a bit of mutual respect between two systems that are both trying to keep the same people alive.
