# Bright Side–Style Video Script #3 (Money / Everyday Finance Genre)

**Working title:** 10 Money Habits Quietly Keeping You Broke
**Alt titles (A/B thumbnails):**
- "10 Money Mistakes You're Probably Making Right Now"
- "If You Do This With Money, Stop"
- "The Broke Habits Nobody Warns You About"

**Target length:** ~9 minutes
**Format:** Voiceover + flat 2D animation / stock b-roll, countdown listicle
**Tone:** Upbeat, friendly, non-judgmental, second-person ("you") — a helpful
friend, never a finance lecture

---

## Why this genre

Money is Bright Side's third reliable pillar after body/health listicles and
"What If…" scenarios. It works for the same reason the body videos do: the
subject is *the viewer's own life*, every item is instantly self-testable, and
the payoff is a small action they can take today. The retention engine is
identical — countdown bumpers, a teased standout item, mid-video comment bait —
but the emotional register shifts from "am I okay?" to "wait, I do that."

**Guardrail that defines this genre:** never shame, never promise riches, never
give specific investment advice. The narrator is on the viewer's side, and the
tone stays "here's the trick" rather than "here's what's wrong with you."

## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN — HOOK (0:00–0:16)
> **[VISUAL:** A wallet on a table. Coins trickle out of a tiny hole in the
> bottom, one at a time, forming a small pile nobody notices.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Nobody goes broke in one dramatic moment. It happens quietly — five dollars here,
a forgotten subscription there, one "I deserve this" at the end of a long week.
The habits doing the damage almost never look like mistakes. They look like
normal life. Today we're counting down ten of them — and number 3 is the one
that fools people who are genuinely good with money.

---

### LOGO STING + PREMISE (0:16–0:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Bright Side logo sting, then a friendly character holding a
> magnifying glass over a receipt.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
So let's find the leaks. Ten money habits that quietly drain your wallet, and the
simple fix for each one. Stay till the end — the last one costs the most, and
almost nobody counts it. Let's get into it.

---

### #10 — Paying for Convenience Without Noticing (0:30–1:15)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "10" bumper. A delivery app checkout screen: item $9,
> then fees stacking up like blocks to $19.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 10 — paying twice for the same thing. That nine-dollar lunch that becomes
nineteen by the time delivery, service fees, and a tip land on top. Convenience is
genuinely worth paying for sometimes — the problem is when you stop seeing the
price. Try this: for one week, look at the final total *before* you tap confirm,
not the item price. Most people find they're comfortable with about half of those
orders — and quietly annoyed by the other half. That other half is your money back.

---

### #9 — The Subscriptions You Forgot You Have (1:15–2:00)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "9". A phone screen scrolling a long list of subscriptions,
> a few glowing red with "LAST USED: 11 MONTHS AGO".**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 9 — the subscription graveyard. The streaming service you signed up for to
watch one show. The app from that motivated Sunday in January. Each one is small
enough to ignore, which is exactly the business model. Open your phone's
subscription list right now — actually, pause the video and do it. Most people
find at least one they'd forgotten. Cancelling a single ten-dollar-a-month ghost
subscription puts a hundred and twenty dollars back in your year, for about
thirty seconds of effort. That's the best hourly rate you'll ever earn.

---

### #8 — Buying It Because It's on Sale (2:00–2:50)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "8". A big red "70% OFF" tag; the character joyfully filling
> a cart, then a closet bulging with unworn tags-still-on clothes.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 8 — letting the discount do your deciding. A seventy-percent-off sign
feels like earning money. It isn't. If you weren't going to buy it at full price,
the sale didn't save you anything — it just found a very cheerful way to spend
your money. The test is one question, asked *before* you look at the discount:
would I want this at the regular price? If the answer's no, the deal isn't a
deal. It's a very good advertisement.

---

### #7 — Keeping Money Where You Can See It (2:50–3:40)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "7". Two jars side by side — one open and constantly
> dipped into, one with a lid, slowly filling.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 7 — savings that live in your spending account. If the money you're
"saving" sits in the same place as the money you're spending, your brain treats
it all as one number. And that number always feels spendable. The fix costs
nothing: move savings into a separate account, ideally one that takes an extra
step to reach. Same money, different address — and suddenly it's much harder to
casually spend on a Tuesday. Friction is your friend here.

---

### MID-VIDEO ENGAGEMENT BAIT (3:40–3:55)
> **[VISUAL:** Comment bubble animation, big "👇" pointing down.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Quick — which one of these have you caught yourself doing this month? Drop the
number in the comments, no judgment, I'll go first: number 9, and it was a
meditation app I have used exactly twice. Okay, back to the countdown — and it's
about to get uncomfortable.

---

### #6 — Waiting Until You "Earn More" (3:55–4:40)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "6". A character on a treadmill; a salary number rises,
> and a matching pile of new expenses rises right alongside it.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 6 — the raise that disappears. It's the most reasonable-sounding plan in
the world: I'll start saving once I'm earning more. But when income goes up,
spending has a habit of going up with it — nicer apartment, better phone, an
upgrade here and there. It's so common that economists have a name for it:
lifestyle inflation. The people who get ahead are usually the ones who let *some*
of a raise raise them, and quietly park the rest before it gets absorbed. You
never miss money you never started spending.

---

### #5 — Not Knowing Your Actual Number (4:40–5:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "5". A character confidently guessing "$400?" while a
> receipt unrolls across the floor showing a much bigger total.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 5 — guessing instead of knowing. Ask most people what they spent last
month and you'll get an estimate. Ask their bank and you'll get a different
answer — usually a bigger one. This isn't a discipline problem, it's a memory
problem: small purchases genuinely don't stick in your head. You don't need a
complicated budget. Just look at one month of transactions, all the way through,
once. That single hour changes more behavior than any spreadsheet, because it's
very hard to un-see your own patterns.

---

### #4 — Emotional Spending in Disguise (5:30–6:15)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "4". A tired character on a couch at 11pm, phone glow on
> their face, cart icon filling up.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 4 — buying a feeling instead of a thing. Rough day, so you order something.
Bored, so you browse. Celebrating, so you treat yourself. None of that is a
character flaw — it works, briefly, which is exactly why it repeats. The trick
isn't willpower, it's timing: put a twenty-four-hour gap between wanting and
buying. Add it to a list, sleep on it, look tomorrow. About half the time the
want simply evaporates overnight — and the other half, you buy it and enjoy it
guilt-free. You're not banning the purchase. You're just letting tomorrow-you vote.

---

### #3 — Being Great at Saving and Terrible at Timing (6:15–7:10)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "3" with a spotlight effect. A neat savings jar labeled
> "SAVED $200/mo" sitting next to a growing stack of interest-charged bills.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 3 — and this is the one that catches genuinely careful people. You can do
everything right on the saving side and still lose ground, if there's
high-interest debt sitting on the other side of the ledger. Money in a basic
savings account grows slowly. A credit card balance grows *fast* — and it never
takes a month off. So diligently saving while carrying an expensive balance can
feel responsible while quietly costing you the difference.

The general principle most financial educators agree on: clear the expensive debt
first, keep a small cushion for emergencies so you don't fall back onto the card,
and *then* build the savings. Same effort, better order. If your situation is
complicated, that's a great conversation to have with an actual advisor — but the
order is the part people miss.

---

### #2 — No Cushion for the Thing That Always Happens (7:10–7:55)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "2". A tire blowing out, a phone screen cracking, a
> refrigerator with X's for eyes — then a calm character with an "EMERGENCY" jar.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number 2 — treating emergencies as surprises. The car, the phone screen, the
appliance, the vet bill: any *specific* one of those is unpredictable, but the
fact that *something* will happen this year isn't. Without a cushion, every one of
those becomes debt, and debt makes the next one worse. Start absurdly small if you
have to — even a few hundred dollars set aside converts a crisis into an
inconvenience. That's the entire job of an emergency fund. It doesn't have to be
impressive. It has to exist.

---

### #1 — Not Charging for Your Own Time (7:55–8:40)
> **[VISUAL:** Number "1". A clock face where the numbers are dollar signs; a
> character saying "sure, no problem" over and over as hours drain away.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
And number 1 — the most expensive habit on this list: never putting a value on
your own time. The extra work you don't get paid for. The raise you never asked
about. The forty-five-minute drive to save three dollars. The hours you'd happily
protect if someone were billing you for them.

Money habits get all the attention because they're easy to count — but time is
the thing you're actually spending, and it's the only account nobody can top up.
Ask what an hour of yours is worth, and a surprising number of these decisions
answer themselves.

---

### RECAP + RIDDLE + CTA OUTRO (8:40–9:05)
> **[VISUAL:** Fast montage of all 10 icons, then the character sealing the hole
> in the wallet from the cold open; subscribe button + bell animation.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
So there they are — ten quiet leaks: hidden fees, ghost subscriptions, fake sales,
savings sitting too close to spending, vanishing raises, guessed numbers,
late-night comfort buys, saving in the wrong order, no cushion, and free time
you never priced. Pick one. Just one. Fix it this week — that's how this actually
works.

None of this is financial advice for your specific situation, by the way. It's
the stuff most people wish someone had mentioned earlier.

Now, a riddle to leave you on: the more of me you save, the more of me you have —
but spend me carelessly and I'm the one thing you can never earn back. What am I?
Drop your guess below.

If this made you check your subscriptions mid-video — be honest — give it a like,
hit subscribe, and tap the bell. Thanks for watching, and go plug a leak.

## Production Notes
- **Pacing:** Swap visuals every 2–4 seconds; the wallet-with-a-hole motif from
  the cold open should return at the outro to close the loop.
- **On-screen text:** Echo each item's key phrase ("GHOST SUBSCRIPTIONS",
  "$120 / YEAR", "24-HOUR RULE") as the narrator says it.
- **Number bumpers:** Big animated countdown number between items — same
  retention driver as the health listicle.
- **The pause beat (#9):** Telling viewers to pause and open their subscription
  list is a deliberate engagement spike — it converts watching into doing and
  reliably fuels comments. Keep it.
- **Music:** Upbeat, light, slightly playful; drop the bed slightly under #1 so
  the closing point lands.
- **Thumbnail:** Wallet with coins falling through a hole + big "10" + a red
  circle on the hole; high-contrast text "QUIETLY BROKE".
- **Retention hook:** The "#3 fools people who are good with money" tease flatters
  the viewer and carries them past the mid-point — the same function as "#3
  surprises everybody" in the body-signals script.
- **Compliance (critical for this genre):** Keep all money claims general and
  behavioral. No specific investments, no rates, no returns, no product names, no
  income promises. Use "most financial educators agree" and "for your specific
  situation, talk to an advisor" — the finance equivalent of the health script's
  "worth a check-up" softening. This keeps the video advertiser-safe and avoids
  the financial-advice policy tripwires on every major platform.
- **Tone guardrail:** Never shame the viewer. Every item pairs the habit with a
  cheap, immediate fix, and the narrator admits to one of the habits at the
  mid-video beat to keep the register friendly rather than superior.
