# Visual Style Reference — Sime Media & Epic History

Breakdown of two reference channels supplied as screenshots, and what we take
from each.

**Source:** two screenshots (Sime Media channel page; Epic History video card).
**Caveat:** analysis of the static design system is read directly from the
images. Motion behaviour is **inferred** from the thumbnail grammar and format
conventions — the actual videos were not viewed.

---

## Channel 1 — Sime Media

`@SimeMedia` · 21.8k subscribers · 79 videos
*"talking about business and finance topics that interest me and maybe interest
you too"*

**This is our niche, already validated.** Business and finance explainers,
faceless, and one video visible at **1.1m views** ("What if you literally can't
pay it back?" — student debt) against a 21.8k subscriber base. That ratio means
the videos travel far beyond the subscriber base, which is exactly the behaviour
of a well-written explainer library.

It is also the single most useful style template we have, because everything it
does is reproducible in CapCut and Canva.

### The design system

**Palette — three colours, no more**

| Role | Colour | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | Near-black charcoal | Every background, every thumbnail |
| Signal | Gold / amber yellow | Hero objects, annotations, logo |
| Paper | Warm cream | Logo lockup, UI card fills |
| Accent | One saturated colour (red) | Used *once* per composition, never twice |

The discipline is the point. Two thumbnails sitting side by side — a gold car and
a gold skull — read as the same channel instantly, despite having nothing in
common as subjects.

**Hero object treatment**

A single real-world object, shot or sourced on black, then colour-graded to
**monochrome gold**. Not a photo of the topic — a *metaphor* for it:

- Gold vintage car → a debt you'll be paying "for 150 years"
- Gold skull in a graduation cap → student loans outliving the borrower

That gold grade is the unifying device. It turns any stock image from any source
into channel-native material, which solves the hardest problem in faceless
production: making assets from ten different places look like one show.

**Typography — quiet, not loud**

Small, light-weight, lowercase or sentence-case sans-serif. Roughly Inter or
Helvetica Now Light. Never bold, never caps, never a drop shadow.

This is the real differentiator. Every other finance thumbnail on the platform
**SCREAMS IN YELLOW CAPS WITH ARROWS**. Sime Media whispers, and in a feed of
shouting the whisper is what stops the scroll. It also reads as credible rather
than as clickbait, which matters enormously for a channel whose product is
accuracy.

**The annotation layer**

Thin leader lines connecting a small label to a point on the object, like a
museum placard or a technical diagram. Paired with asterisked footnotes:

> `*for 150 years`

The asterisk is doing real work — it implies a punchline that the thumbnail
deliberately withholds. That's a curiosity gap built with a single character.

**Fake UI cards**

Clean panels mimicking a banking or app interface:

> `total balance: $590,506`
> `progress: 0%`

Instantly legible, emotionally loaded, and essentially free to produce. A
progress bar sitting at 0% next to a six-figure balance tells the entire story of
the video before a word is spoken.

**Facelessness as branding**

The avatar is a gold human silhouette with the eyes struck out by white bars.
The channel doesn't hide that there's no face — it makes the absence the logo.
The wordmark is lowercase "sime media" with a hand-drawn circle scribbled around
it, which adds the one human, imperfect gesture in an otherwise clinical system.

### Inferred motion

Given this design language, the videos almost certainly run on:

- Slow push-in / drift on static hero images (Ken Burns, but restrained)
- Elements sliding or fading in on the narration beat, not continuously
- Numbers counting up in the UI cards
- Leader lines drawing on, left to right
- Hard cuts on black between sections

**No character animation. No motion graphics rigs. No After Effects required.**
Every one of those moves is a keyframe pair in CapCut. This is a style built
deliberately for a one-person pipeline, which is why it's the right model for us.

## Channel 2 — Epic History

*"The Greatest General in History? Alexander the Great (All Parts)"* —
**28m views, 8 years old.**

A completely different production tier, and honest about it.

### The design system

- **Animated territorial maps.** Red and blue fills spreading across an
  aged-parchment map as the narration advances. This is the channel's signature
  and its main cost centre.
- **Classical artwork** — oil paintings and engravings, given subtle parallax so
  a still image breathes.
- **Bold condensed uppercase display type** for titles. The opposite of Sime
  Media's whisper, and correct for the genre — historical epic wants weight.
- **Date-box device:** `334 – 323 BC` in a black chip. Instantly orients the
  viewer and does the same job as a chapter marker.
- **Muted historical palette** — ochre, oxblood, navy, parchment. Nothing
  saturated, nothing modern.

### What it actually costs

Map animation at this quality is After Effects work — days per video, not hours.
Epic History is a small studio, not one person with CapCut. **We cannot and
should not try to reproduce this look on a weekly cadence.** Attempting it is the
fastest way to miss six straight publish slots.

### What we take from it anyway

Two structural lessons, both free:

1. **28 million views on an eight-year-old video.** This is the evergreen
   argument made concrete — the exact reason the channel plan avoided
   news-driven topics. That video has been earning for eight years.
2. **The "(All Parts)" compilation.** Publish parts individually, then stitch
   them into one long-form cut. Costs nothing but an export, and the compilation
   often outperforms every part it's made of, because session watch time is what
   the algorithm rewards hardest.
3. **The date-chip device** transfers directly. `1994` or `Q3 2008` in a black
   box does the same orienting work for a business story that `334 BC` does for
   Alexander.

## What We Adopt

**Take Sime Media's grammar wholesale — but not its palette.**

Their system is the correct production model for a solo faceless channel: a
locked three-colour scheme, one metaphorical hero object per composition graded
into a signature tone, quiet small type, thin annotation lines, fake UI panels,
and motion that never exceeds a slow push and a timed fade.

But gold-on-black in the business-explainer niche is now *theirs*. Copying it
makes us a knockoff of a channel our audience already watches. We copy the
method and change the signature colour.

### Palette options for "Priced In"

| Option | Ground | Signature | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — Receipt** | Warm off-white / receipt paper | Deep oxblood red objects, black type | Inverts the whole niche's dark-mode default. Highest contrast in a feed. Strong first choice. |
| **B — Blueprint** | Deep navy / ink | Cyan-white line work, paper-white type | Technical, diagrammatic, suits "how it works" framing |
| **C — Ledger** | Near-black | Desaturated sage green, cream type | Closest to Sime Media. Safest, least distinctive. |

Recommendation: **A**. Everyone in this niche is on black. A cream, receipt-toned
channel is instantly identifiable in a feed of dark thumbnails, and "receipt"
is on-concept for a channel about what things actually cost.

### The reproducible recipe

1. Source one metaphorical object per video (Pexels / Pixabay / Unsplash).
2. Cut it out, drop it on the ground colour, apply the **same** colour grade
   preset every single time. Save it as a Canva/CapCut preset on video one.
3. Add small light-weight sentence-case type. Never caps. Never a shadow.
4. Add one thin leader line with an asterisked label that withholds the answer.
5. Where a number carries the story, put it in a plain UI card.
6. Motion: slow push-in, elements fade in on the beat, numbers count up, lines
   draw on. Nothing else.
7. Visual changes every 3–5 seconds — but *within* this system, so it never
   looks like stock-footage soup.

The whole point: this survives 52 videos made by one person, and it looks
deliberate rather than cheap.
