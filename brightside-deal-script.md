# Bright Side–Style Video Script #3 ("Deal" Genre — Consumer Psychology)

**Working title:** 8 Sneaky Tricks Stores Use to Make You Think It's a Deal
**Alt titles (A/B thumbnails):**
- "Why That 'Deal' Isn't Actually a Deal"
- "Stores Don't Want You to Know These 8 Price Tricks"
- "The Psychology Behind Every Sale Tag"

**Target length:** ~9 minutes
**Format:** Voiceover + flat 2D animation / stock b-roll, countdown listicle
**Tone:** Friendly insider, lightly conspiratorial but never cynical, second-person ("you")

---

## Why this genre

The "hidden tricks" explainer is Bright Side's third pillar after body/health
listicles and "What If…" scenarios. It borrows the countdown structure of the
listicle but swaps medical curiosity for **the reveal**: every item shows the
viewer something they've seen a thousand times and never noticed. Retention comes
from recognition ("wait, my store does that") rather than alarm, and the payoff
beat is empowerment — the viewer leaves feeling smarter, not scammed.

**Tone guardrail:** Stores are clever, not evil. Keep it playful. No naming real
retailers, no accusations of illegality — this is psychology, not exposé.

## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN — HOOK (0:00–0:16)
> **[VISUAL:** A shopper walks into a bright store. A giant red "50% OFF" tag
> swings toward camera. Freeze frame; the tag peels back to reveal a tiny
> price sticker underneath.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
You walk in for one thing. You walk out with a bag, a receipt you don't want to
look at, and a warm little feeling that you *won*. Here's the uncomfortable part:
that feeling was designed. Every sign, every price tag, even the floor under your
feet was arranged by someone who studies how you decide. And trick number one is
sitting on the shelf right in front of you — you just read right past it.

---

### LOGO STING + PREMISE (0:16–0:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Bright Side logo sting. A price tag graphic splits into eight
> numbered fragments.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Today: eight tricks that turn an ordinary price into an irresistible deal — and
how to spot every single one of them. Stick around for number one, because it's
the trick that makes all the others work.

---

### #8 — THE PRICE THAT ENDS IN 9 (0:30–1:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Big number bumper "8". Two tags side by side: $20.00 and $19.99.
> A cartoon brain lights up at the second one.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number eight: the number nine.
Nineteen ninety-nine. Four ninety-nine. Nine ninety-five. You know it's basically
twenty dollars. Your brain knows it too — but your brain reads left to right, and
it grabs that first digit before the rest catches up. Researchers call it the
*left-digit effect*, and it's why a one-cent difference can feel like a whole
different price bracket.

Here's the aha: the trick isn't the cent you save. It's the digit you see first.
Nineteen starts with a one. Twenty starts with a two. Same money — different
shelf in your head.

**On-screen text:** "$19.99 → your brain reads 'nineteen'"

---

### #7 — THE PRICE THAT'S THERE TO LOSE (1:30–2:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "7". Three coffee sizes: small $3, large $7,
> and a comically huge one at $7.50 that nobody buys.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number seven: the option nobody's supposed to buy.
Put two things side by side and you'll agonize. Add a third that's *slightly
worse than one of them*, and suddenly the choice makes itself.

That giant size at barely more than the large? It's not there for you to buy.
It's there to make the large look generous. It's a decoy — a price that exists
purely to lose, so another one can win.

Once you see it, you'll spot decoys everywhere: phone plans, streaming tiers,
popcorn at the movies. Somebody in that lineup is taking a fall.

---

### #6 — THE PRICE IT "USED TO BE" (2:30–3:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "6". A tag reading "WAS $80 — NOW $40" with the
> $80 slowly fading into a question mark.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number six: the crossed-out price.
Was eighty, now forty. Your brain doesn't judge forty dollars on its own — it
judges forty *against eighty*. That first number is called an **anchor**, and once
it's in your head, everything after it gets measured against it.

Here's the quiet question worth asking: how long was it actually eighty? A week?
A day? Anywhere? The discount is real only if the anchor was.

Quick pause — what's the wildest "was / now" tag you've ever seen? Drop the numbers
in the comments. I've seen one where the "before" price only existed for a single
weekend.

---

### #5 — THE CLOCK YOU DIDN'T ASK FOR (3:30–4:20)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "5". A countdown timer ticking beside a product;
> "ONLY 3 LEFT!" flashing red.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number five: urgency.
Ends tonight. Only three left. Twelve people are looking at this right now.

Scarcity does something specific to decision-making — it shortcuts it. When a
choice feels like it's about to disappear, the part of you that compares prices
gets shoved aside by the part of you that doesn't want to miss out.

The tell is simple: a genuine deal survives a night's sleep. A trick usually
doesn't want you to have one.

---

### #4 — THE MATH THAT ISN'T MATH (4:20–5:10)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "4". A "BUY 2 GET 1 FREE" banner; a calculator
> cheerfully showing "33% off" beside it.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number four: free that isn't free.
Buy two, get one free sounds like a gift. Run the numbers and it's a discount of
about a third — respectable, but nothing magic. "Free," though, is the most
persuasive word in retail. It skips the arithmetic entirely.

Same with "spend fifty, get free shipping" when your cart says forty-two. That
eight-dollar top-up feels like saving. It's spending.

**On-screen text:** "BUY 2 GET 1 FREE = ~33% OFF"

---

### MID-VIDEO ENGAGEMENT + TEASE (5:10–5:25)
> **[VISUAL:** A shopping cart rolling past a glowing door marked "THE TOP 3".**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Still with me? Good — because the top three aren't about price tags at all.
They're about the building you're standing in. And number one is the reason you
can never find the milk.

---

### #3 — THE ROOM ITSELF (5:25–6:20)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "3". Overhead store map: entrance, a winding path,
> essentials tucked at the very back.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number three: the layout.
Milk, eggs, bread — the things almost everyone came for — tend to live as far from
the door as possible. Not to annoy you. To walk you past everything else.

Wide, slow aisles at the entrance ease you out of rush mode. The most profitable
items sit at adult eye level; the ones aimed at kids sit lower, exactly where small
eyes land. Even the music has a job: slower tempo, slower steps, longer visit.

You're not shopping in a room. You're shopping in a route.

---

### #2 — THE LIMIT THAT ISN'T A LIMIT (6:20–7:10)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "2". A shelf sign: "LIMIT 6 PER CUSTOMER" with
> shoppers loading up six.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
Number two: "limit six per customer."
There's no shortage. There's rarely even a rule being enforced. But that number
does two things at once: it whispers *this is in demand*, and it quietly plants a
quantity in your head. People who came for one walk out with four.

It's an anchor again — wearing a different hat. Any number a store shows you is a
suggestion, even when it's dressed up as a restriction.

---

### #1 — THE FEELING OF WINNING (7:10–8:10)
> **[VISUAL:** Number bumper "1". A shopper at checkout; the receipt total is
> ignored while a glowing "YOU SAVED $23!" line pulses.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
And number one — the trick that powers all the others: the receipt that tells you
how much you *saved*.

Notice what just happened. You went in to spend money. You came out being
congratulated. The feeling of a good deal is its own product, and it's the one
thing on your receipt you can't return.

That's why the nine, the decoy, the crossed-out eighty, the countdown clock and
the winding aisles all point in one direction: not at a cheaper price, but at a
better *story* about the price.

Here's the fix, and it's almost boringly simple. Ask one question at the shelf:
**"Would I want this at this price if there were no sale at all?"**
If yes — enjoy it, genuinely. If you hesitate, the deal was never the product.
It was the discount.

---

### THE REASSURANCE (8:10–8:30)
> **[VISUAL:** Shopper walking out calmly with exactly one item, smiling.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
And look — none of this makes stores the villain. Everything here is ordinary
persuasion, the same way a menu photo or a movie trailer is. The advantage is just
that most people never learn the moves. As of about two minutes ago, you did.

---

### RECAP + RIDDLE + CTA OUTRO (8:30–8:55)
> **[VISUAL:** Rapid montage of all eight tags; character winking; subscribe +
> bell animation.**]**

**NARRATOR:**
So: the nine that reads as a one, the decoy built to lose, the anchor that may never
have existed, the clock, the "free" that's really thirty-three percent, the route
you're walked through, the limit that's a suggestion — and a receipt that hands you
a trophy.

Before you go, a riddle: the more of me you're offered, the more you tend to spend.
What am I? Answer in the comments — you already know it.

If this changed how the next price tag looks to you, hit like, subscribe, and ring
the bell. Stay curious — and spend on purpose.

## Production Notes
- **Bumpers:** Standard big-number countdown cards (8 → 1), styled as price tags
  rather than plain numerals to tie the visual motif together.
- **Reveal rhythm:** Every item follows number → familiar sight → the hidden
  mechanism → one-line "aha". Never explain the mechanism before showing the sight.
- **Escalation logic:** Items 8–4 are tag-level tricks, 3–1 escalate to the store
  and then to the shopper's own emotions — the widening scope is the retention curve.
- **Tone guardrail:** Playful, never accusatory. No real retailer names, no claims
  of deception or illegality — frame everything as psychology and ordinary marketing.
- **On-screen text:** Echo the short numeric beats ("$19.99 → 'nineteen'",
  "BUY 2 GET 1 FREE = ~33% OFF", "LIMIT 6").
- **Thumbnail:** Giant red "50% OFF" tag half-peeled to show a shrugging character
  behind it; text: "IT'S NOT A DEAL".
- **Music:** Light, curious, slightly sneaky pizzicato; small sting on each reveal,
  warm resolve at the reassurance beat.
- **Fact hygiene:** Left-digit effect, anchoring, decoy effect, scarcity and store
  layout are all well-documented consumer-psychology staples — keep soft framing
  ("tend to", "often", "about") and avoid citing specific studies on camera.
