## The Deciding Constraint

No domain expertise removes more options than the audience or budget does.

Two whole categories are out:

- **Advice content is out.** Personal finance, health, investing, and legal are
  what YouTube classifies as **YMYL — Your Money or Your Life**. These get held to
  a higher bar on both ranking and monetization, they need a credibility bridge we
  can't honestly write, and being wrong causes real harm to real people. "How to
  pay off your debt" is off the table. So is anything prescriptive about money,
  medicine, or law.
- **Experience content is out.** Anything whose value comes from *having done it*
  — building a business, losing weight, trading. We'd be fabricating a person.

What's left, and what's actually the strongest play here, is **explanatory
journalism**: third-person analysis of how businesses and money systems work.
No advice, no persona, no lived experience required. Every claim is
researchable and verifiable. The credibility comes from the research, not from
the narrator.

This is not a downgrade. It's the highest-performing faceless format on the
platform, and it happens to be the one our constraints point at.

### Why not #1, the obvious one

It has the RPM, and it's exactly what the source thread did. It's also the single
most saturated corner of YouTube right now, filled with channels running the
identical stack we'd be running, and it's YMYL. We'd be entering the most
crowded room with the least differentiation and the highest policy risk. The
thread's author got in eight months ago; that window is shut.

### Why not #3, the safe one

Genuinely good and nearly won. Endless topics, no YMYL, high repeatability. It
loses on one thing: **shelf life.** A video about a tool released this month is
dead in a year. Every video decays, so the library never compounds — you're
running to stand still. Business explainers stay watchable for years, which is
what turns 52 videos into an asset instead of a treadmill.

## Recommendation: Business & Money Explainers

**The channel answers one repeating question: *how does this actually make
money?*** Companies, industries, pricing schemes, and the systems underneath
them.

**Why it wins here:**

- **RPM.** Sits in the finance/business advertiser pool — $12–22 US — without
  being advice.
- **No credibility required.** The format is third-person and factual. The
  narrator is a guide, not an authority. Nothing to fake.
- **Infinite supply.** Every company, every industry, every pricing quirk is a
  video. We will not run out.
- **Evergreen.** "Why printer ink costs more than champagne" performs the same
  in 2029 as today. The library compounds.
- **Differentiable through writing alone** — which is the one input we control
  completely and can make genuinely excellent.
- **Sponsor-friendly.** Business audiences attract real sponsors, which is
  usually a bigger line than AdSense by year two.

**Real risks, stated plainly:**

- **Top-end competition is strong.** Established channels own this format. We
  compete in the middle and in the gaps — the unglamorous industries nobody
  bothers with.
- **Accuracy is the whole product.** One fabricated statistic ends the channel's
  credibility. Every number in every script needs a real source. This is the
  actual work, and it is not optional.
- **Novelty of the topic can't carry a bad script.** It never could.

## Production Standard

Non-negotiable, because accuracy is the entire product:

1. **Every number gets a source.** Annual reports, SEC filings, and reputable
   reporting. If a figure can't be sourced, it doesn't go in the script.
2. **Estimates get labeled as estimates.** "Analysts put it around…" not "It is."
3. **No advice, ever.** We explain how a thing works. We never tell a viewer what
   to do with their money.
4. **Voice settings locked** — same voice ID, speed, and stability on all 52.
5. **Altered-content disclosure ticked** on every upload, from video one.
6. **Same publish slot weekly**, held for six months without exception.
