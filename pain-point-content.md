# Content Plan — Ranked by Shared Pain

Replaces the launch order in `niche-analysis.md`.

The original order was built on curiosity and research burden. This one is built
on **how many people have personally felt the thing.**

---

## The Principle

**The content is the hook.** No device, no gimmick, no format trick.

Company Man's opener works because it's a *number the viewer can't explain* —
and the fact that it happened to them is what makes them stay. We don't need a
signature visual device on top of that. We need topics where the viewer has
already lived the story before we tell it.

### Evidence this is the right filter

**Sime Media's biggest video — 1.1M views against a 21.8K subscriber base — was
about student loans.** Not a clever format. A pain point millions of people share.
That ratio only happens when a video reaches far outside its own audience, and
shared pain is what carries it there.

---

## The Ranking Test

A topic scores if it passes all four:

1. **Have they felt it personally?** Not "is it interesting" — have they stood at
   the till, opened the bill, seen the number.
2. **Do they already suspect they're being had?** We confirm a suspicion rather
   than introduce one. Confirmation travels; education doesn't.
3. **Is it visible?** Something they can point at in their own life this week.
4. **Is it pure mechanism?** We explain how it works. We never advise. This is
   the YMYL guardrail and it is not negotiable.

### Why video 1 changed

Printer ink was my pick on curiosity. **Shrinkflation is the better opener** on
every criterion that matters:

- **Everyone has noticed it.** Not "learned about it" — *noticed it*, in their own
  kitchen. The cereal box is thinner. The crisp packet has more air. The chocolate
  bar has a bigger gap.
- **They already suspect it.** We're confirming, not teaching.
- **It's visible today.** They can check their own cupboard while watching.
- **It's pure mechanism.** No advice, no YMYL exposure, nothing to get wrong
  medically or financially.
- **It's a perfect stick figure video.** A figure holding a box. The box shrinks.
  The price tag doesn't move. That's the entire visual argument in one shot, and
  it works as a thumbnail.

Printer ink stays in the run at #7. It's a good video. It just isn't the one that
makes a stranger stop.

## The Hook Rule

Every video opens on a number the viewer cannot explain, about something that has
happened to them.

**Video 1:**

> "The box on your shelf holds nine percent less than it did three years ago. The
> price didn't change. Nobody told you. And it's not inflation — it's a decision
> somebody made in a meeting."

No greeting. No channel name. No "in today's video."

The last clause is doing the real work: *somebody decided this.* That converts a
vague economic grievance into a story with people in it — which is the
Improvement Pill move, and it's what the stick figures are for.

## The Tone — Nostalgia, Memory, History

This is the emotional engine, and it's what turns a generic explainer channel
into one with a voice.

**The channel's real sentence is: *"It didn't used to be like this. Here's the
year it changed, and who changed it."***

### Why this works so well with the pain-point filter

Every pain point on the list above is secretly a **loss**. The box was bigger. The
insurance was cheaper. The fee didn't exist. The rent was payable. The viewer
isn't just annoyed — they're **remembering something that's gone**, and they've
never had anyone explain where it went.

Nostalgia and grievance are the same feeling pointed in two directions. Running
both at once is far stronger than either alone:

- **Nostalgia** gets the click and the comment — "I remember this"
- **History** earns the credibility — a specific year, a specific decision
- **Pain** is the payoff — now you know who did it

An explainer makes you informed. This makes you **vindicated.** That's a much
more shareable feeling.

### What it changes structurally

The seven-section template gains a spine: **then → the moment it changed → now.**

| Section | With the nostalgia layer |
|---|---|
| The fall | The number, and the year it was different |
| The character | The figure, **in the past** — the world as it was |
| The setup | How it worked when it worked |
| **The turn** | **The specific year, the specific decision, the specific room** |
| The mechanism | How the change propagated |
| The scale | Everything else that changed the same way |
| The callback | Back to the opening — now dated |

The turn is no longer just "here's a number that doesn't add up." It's **"in
1987, someone in a meeting decided this,"** which is a far better story beat and
gives every video a villain that isn't a conspiracy — just a decision.

### What it changes visually

The receipt-paper palette was already half-vintage. Push it:

- **Aged paper texture** on the cream ground — subtle, not a filter
- **Date chips** — the Epic History device, now load-bearing. `1987` in a black
  box, exactly as they use `334 BC`. It orients the viewer and marks the turn.
- **Era objects** in the stick figure world — the old packaging, the old price
  tag, the old logo. Rendered in the same vector line style so nothing breaks.
- **Then/now split frames** — same composition, two dates. The single most
  efficient shot the channel can make, and a ready-made thumbnail.

### What it changes about the name

`Priced In` is accurate but cold — it doesn't carry memory. Better fits:

| Name | Read |
|---|---|
| **Used To Be** | Simple, evocative, says the whole thesis in two words. First choice. |
| **Back When** | Warm, conversational, memory-first |
| **The Year It Changed** | Most literal, strong for a series format |
| **The Long Receipt** | Still works — a receipt is a historical document |

### Video 1, reframed

> "The cereal box in your cupboard holds nine percent less than the one you grew
> up with. Same box. Same price. Same shelf. Nobody announced it. And it didn't
> happen slowly — there was a year, and there was a meeting."

Same facts. But now it's about **a thing you remember**, which is what makes a
stranger stop.
